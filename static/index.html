Implicit association testing

Measuring what people don't say.

An implicit association test measures how quickly you sort things into categories when those categories share a key. If two ideas are already linked in your head, pairing them is easy and you are fast. If they aren't, you hesitate — by somewhere between forty and two hundred milliseconds. That gap is the measurement.

This runs the real thing: the seven-block design from Greenwald, Nosek and Banaji (2003), scored with their improved D algorithm, with the data-quality exclusions applied, a bootstrap interval around the estimate, and a written summary produced by a language model that is never allowed to see a raw latency.

1 · Choose a study

Advanced — custom brands and a fixed seed

The seed decides the trial order and both counterbalancing factors. Two sessions with the same seed present exactly the same stimuli in exactly the same order — which is what makes a session re-analysable rather than merely repeatable.

2 · Before you start

You will see brand names and descriptive words one at a time. Press E for the category shown on the left and I for the one on the right. Go as fast as you can while staying accurate — a wrong key shows a red ✗ and you must correct it before the next trial.

Use a physical keyboard, sit somewhere without interruptions, and keep this tab in the foreground. The task detects and reports it if you switch away.

ⓘ What is recorded. Your response times and keypresses for this session, plus your display's refresh rate. No name, no email, no IP address, no cookie, no tracker. Data is stored against a random session ID so the result page can be reloaded, and it is used to build the comparison distribution shown at the end. Nothing identifies you. Close the tab before finishing and nothing is stored at all.

The worked example runs a synthetic respondent through the same pipeline so you can see the full report without doing 190 trials. It is labelled as simulated everywhere it appears.

Sensitivity to the scoring choice
